1The idea in one page

GRPO-style training has many knobs: the advantage rule, the group size $G$, the number of prompts per step $B$, the step size $\eta$, the KL strength $\beta$ and direction, the response length $L$, and how the model shares parameters across prompts. Sweeping them one by one gives a hyperparameter search, not an understanding. Our claim is that they act through a few effective quantities, and that the theory papers each describe one of them.

The equation behind everything

For binary rewards and any advantage rule that depends only on the group's rewards, the expected update on a prompt $x$ is a scalar times the gradient of its success probability: $$\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat g_x\big] \;=\; W_G(p_x)\,\nabla_\theta\, p_x, \qquad W_G(p) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[A \mid r=1] - \mathbb{E}[A \mid r=0].$$ This holds for any parameterization: a table of logits, a shared MLP, any $L$. Only the scalar $W_G(p)$, the advantage gap, depends on the rule and on $G$.

From this one equation, the knobs fall into four roles:

RoleGoverned byPaper it generalizes
What is optimizedThe shape of $W_G(p)$ across prompts (rule, $G$)Thrampoulidis et al.
Where training stopsThe balance $W_G(p) = \beta \times$ KL pullMroueh
Whether training is stableThe per-prompt effective step $s = \eta\, W_G / B$ times how much prompts interfereSuk & Duan
How noisy it is$G$ and $B$, and the chance $\varphi$ that a group is informativeBay & Yearick, Nie et al.

Two consequences make this more than bookkeeping. First, the scale of $W$ is absorbed by the step size, so many configurations that look different are provably the same, which collapses the grid. Second, a pilot shows that the main failure mode is not "SGD is noisy" but a specific mechanism: prompts get pushed into a deterministic wrong answer, where no group ever contains a correct sample and the prompt can never recover. That mechanism links step size, group size, KL strength and parameter sharing in testable ways.

1.5The advantage gap in detail

This section unpacks the boxed equation above: what each piece is, why it holds, what it lets us say, and where it stops being true. The full proof and its numerical check are in Section 6.1; definitions of every symbol are in Section 3.

The setup

Fix one prompt $x$. Sample a group of $G$ responses $y_1,\dots,y_G$ from the current policy $\pi_\theta$, score each with the verifier ($r_i=1$ if correct, $0$ if not), turn the rewards into advantages $A_1,\dots,A_G$ with some rule, and form the update direction

$$\widehat g_x \;=\; \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} A_i\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x).$$

$\widehat g_x$ is random because the group is random. The identity is a statement about its average over groups, $\mathbb{E}[\widehat g_x]$: the direction training moves in on this prompt, on average.

Why it holds, in three steps

  1. Split each sample by whether it was correct. With probability $p$ the sample is correct, with probability $1-p$ it is wrong. Because the advantage $A_i$ depends on $y_i$ only through $r_i$ (and on the other, independent samples), the average splits into a "correct" term and a "wrong" term: $$\mathbb{E}\big[A\,\nabla\log\pi\big] = p\;\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]\;\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}1] \;+\;(1-p)\;\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]\;\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}0].$$
  2. Average score of correct and wrong samples. The score $\nabla\log\pi$ averaged over correct samples points toward making correct answers more likely, and over wrong samples it points the opposite way, with the same total size: $$\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}1] = \frac{\nabla p}{p},\qquad \mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}0] = -\frac{\nabla p}{1-p}.$$ (These follow from $\mathbb{E}[r\,\nabla\log\pi]=\nabla p$ and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0$.)
  3. The probabilities cancel. Substituting, the $p$ and $1-p$ cancel exactly: $$\mathbb{E}[A\,\nabla\log\pi] = \mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]\,\nabla p \;-\; \mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]\,\nabla p = \big(\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]\big)\,\nabla p.$$ Averaging over the $G$ samples in the group changes nothing, since they are identically distributed.

So $W_G(p)$ is simply how much more the rule rewards a correct answer than a wrong one, on average. It is a number, not a vector, and it depends only on $p$, $G$ and the rule.

A worked example

Take $G=4$ and a prompt with $p=0.2$. For a fixed sample, average its advantage over the other three samples' rewards, once assuming it is correct and once assuming it is wrong:

Rule$\mathbb{E}[A\mid r=1]$$\mathbb{E}[A\mid r=0]$$W_4(0.2)$
Mean-only, $A_i=r_i-\bar r$0.600−0.1500.750
RLOO, $A_i=r_i-\text{mean of others}$0.800−0.2001.000
GRPO, $(r_i-\bar r)/\mathrm{std}$1.326−0.3321.658

For mean-only by hand: if the sample is correct, $\bar r=(1+3p)/4$ on average, so $\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]=1-(1+3p)/4=0.6$; if it is wrong, $\bar r=3p/4$, so $\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]=-3p/4=-0.15$. The gap is $0.75=(G-1)/G$, and the $p$ terms cancel in general, so mean-only's gap does not depend on $p$. RLOO's gap is exactly $1$ for the same reason. GRPO's does depend on $p$, because the standard deviation it divides by depends on how many samples were correct.

What it lets us say

  1. All rules agree on the direction. On average every rule moves $\theta$ along $\nabla p_x$, toward a higher success rate on that prompt. Mean-only, RLOO and GRPO differ only by the scalar $W_G(p_x)$, which acts as a per-prompt step-size multiplier.
  2. It sets the effective step. Writing $p=\sigma(z)$ for the logit $z$ of success, $\nabla p = p(1-p)\nabla z$, so one step moves the logit by about $$\Delta z_x \approx \frac{\eta}{B}\,W_G(p_x)\,p_x(1-p_x)\,\|\nabla z_x\|^2.$$ Step size $\eta$, prompts per step $B$, group size and rule (through $W$), and the current difficulty $p$ all enter through this one quantity. This is what H3 tests instead of a grid over each knob.
  3. The scale is absorbed by $\eta$. A constant $W$ is indistinguishable from a different step size: mean-only with $\eta$ is RLOO with $\eta(G-1)/G$, in expectation. Only the shape of $W$ in $p$ changes behaviour (H1).
  4. The shape decides which prompts get pushed hardest. As $G\to\infty$, GRPO's $W\to 1/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, so $W\,p(1-p)\to\sqrt{p(1-p)}$. At $p=0.05$ that is $0.218$, against $0.0475$ for mean-only: GRPO pushes hard prompts about 4.6 times harder. That helps them escape, but it also lowers the step size at which training breaks, which is what the pilot showed (Section 7).
  5. It defines an objective. Since $\mathbb{E}[\widehat g]=W_G(p)\nabla p=\nabla F_G(p)$ with $F_G(p)=\int_0^p W_G(u)\,du$, each rule is gradient ascent on its own surrogate $F_G$. Mean-only, RLOO and GRPO at $G=2$ climb $p$ itself (pass@1); GRPO at large $G$ climbs $2\arcsin\sqrt p$. Group size therefore changes what GRPO optimizes (H2).
  6. It sets where training stops. With a KL penalty, training stops where the push $W_G(p)$ balances the pull $\beta\times$(KL gradient). That gives the fixed points tested in H4.

What it does not cover

  • Noise. It is a statement about the average update. Two rules with the same $W$ can have very different variance; Section 7.3 shows GRPO's per-sample signal-to-noise rises with $G$ while mean-only's stays flat.
  • Interference. It is per prompt. With shared parameters, a step on prompt $x$ also moves every other prompt, through $\nabla p_x\cdot\nabla p_{x'}$. That cross-talk is the missing piece in Section 6.5, and the source of the collapse seen in the pilot.
  • Its assumptions. Binary rewards; independent samples in a group; advantages computed from the group's rewards only; and the summed log-probability of the response. It fails with PPO-style clipping, with per-token length normalization when lengths vary, and with non-binary rewards. A KL term adds its own separate gradient on top.

Where it comes from, and what is ours

Credit

The fact that the expected update is a scalar function of $p$ times $\nabla p$ is not new. Davis & Recht (2025) show that common rules with binary rewards are, in expectation, ascent on a monotone transform of $p$ (GRPO's is $\arcsin\sqrt p$). Mroueh (2025) writes GRPO as a contrastive loss with $p$-dependent weights. Thrampoulidis et al. show that reshaping the advantage is equivalent to maximizing a surrogate reward. We should not write that we "introduce" the identity.

What we can claim: the name advantage gap and the one-line formula $W=\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]$ that computes it for any rule; using it to put the three papers on one axis; the effective-step view that ties $\eta$, $B$, $G$, the rule and sharing together; and the experiments that test its predictions. A safe phrasing for the write-up: "Following Davis & Recht (2025) and Mroueh (2025), we write the expected update of any group-baseline rule as $W_G(p)\nabla p$ and call $W_G$ the advantage gap." Check whether Davis & Recht state the finite-$G$ form or only the large-$G$ limit before writing the related-work sentence.

3Definitions from the basics

3.1 Prompts, responses, policies

Definition

A prompt $x$ is the input. A response $y$ is the output. A policy $\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ is a probability distribution over responses for each prompt, with parameters $\theta$. Training changes $\theta$.

In our code, prompts and responses are plain arrays, not text. The task family has two parameterizations (Section 4), and the format differs slightly between them:

Shared (MLP, synthetic_rl/)Tabular / bandit (main.py)
One prompt $x$ A float vector in $\mathbb{R}^d$ (default $d=16$), drawn from $\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. It has no meaning beyond being the input to the hidden rule. Just an identity: a prompt index $j\in\{0,\dots,n-1\}$, or equivalently a one-hot vector of length $n$. main.py uses a single prompt.
One response $y$ An integer sequence of length $L$, each entry a token in $\{0,\dots,K-1\}$. Example with $K=4$, $L=2$: [3, 1]. A single integer in $\{0,\dots,K-1\}$ (the chosen answer), so $L=1$.
Correct answer $y^\star(x)$ An integer sequence of length $L$, computed from $x$ by the hidden rule. Only one sequence is correct. A fixed integer (index 0 in main.py).
Reward $r$ 1.0 if the response equals the correct answer at every position, else 0.0.

Batched shapes in synthetic_rl, with $B$ prompts per step and $G$ responses per prompt:

In the bandit, the same objects shrink to a vector of $K$ logits, a group of $G$ integers, and $G$ rewards.

What $\theta$ is

  • Bandit / tabular: $\theta$ is just the logits, $K$ numbers per prompt. Changing one prompt's logits cannot affect any other prompt.
  • Shared MLP: $\theta$ is the weights of a network $d \to 128 \to 128 \to L\cdot K$ with ReLU activations. For $d=16$, $K=4$, $L=2$ that is 19,720 numbers shared by all prompts. The last layer starts at zero, so the initial policy is exactly uniform: $\pi_\theta(y_\ell\mid x)=1/K$ for every prompt and position.

A policy is a distribution, not a choice

The policy outputs probabilities; responses are sampled from them, so the same prompt can get different responses on different draws. That randomness is what lets a group of $G$ responses contain both correct and wrong answers, and it is why the success probability $p$ (next section) is the natural quantity to track rather than whether "the" answer is right. A policy that puts all its mass on one response is called deterministic; its entropy is zero, and Section 6.5 shows why reaching that state on a wrong answer is the main way training fails.

3.2 Verifier, reward, success probability

Definition

A verifier checks a response and returns a binary reward $r(x,y)\in\{0,1\}$. RLVR (reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards) is the framework of training a policy on such rewards; GRPO and RLOO are algorithms within it. The success probability on prompt $x$ is $$p_\theta(x) = \Pr_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)=1\big] = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(x,y)\big].$$ The initial success rate $p_0$ is its value when RL starts, that is, for the base policy.

The verifier in our task

  • Exact match. verify returns 1 if the sampled sequence equals the correct sequence at every one of the $L$ positions, else 0. There is no partial credit: getting $L-1$ tokens right earns the same 0 as getting none right. This is what makes rewards sparse as $L$ grows.
  • Deterministic and free. The same response always gets the same reward, and checking costs nothing. Unlike a learned reward model in RLHF, it cannot be fooled or "hacked"; the only way to earn reward is to be correct.
  • One correct answer per prompt. Our task has exactly one correct sequence. (Real math problems often have many correct solutions; supporting that would need a multi-solution variant of the task.)

Computing $p$ exactly

Because tokens are drawn independently given $x$ and only one sequence is correct, $p$ is a product of the probabilities the policy gives the correct token at each position:

$$p_\theta(x) = \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \pi_\theta\big(y^\star_\ell(x)\mid x\big).$$

Example with $K=4$, $L=2$: if the policy puts probability $0.5$ on the correct first token and $0.4$ on the correct second token, then $p = 0.5\times0.4 = 0.2$. A uniform policy would have $p = (1/4)^2 = 0.0625$. In the bandit, $p$ is just the probability of the correct answer, $\pi(y^\star)$. In code this is Policy.success_probability; no sampling is needed.

What a group of rewards looks like

Each sampled response is correct with probability $p$, independently, so a reward is a Bernoulli($p$) coin flip and the number of correct responses in a group of $G$ is Binomial($G$, $p$). The group mean $\bar r$ is an unbiased estimate of $p$. With $p=0.2$ and $G=8$: on average $1.6$ responses are correct, the whole group is wrong with probability $0.8^8\approx0.17$, and the whole group is right with probability $0.2^8\approx3\times10^{-6}$. These all-equal groups carry no learning signal (Section 3.4).

Per-prompt and average success

$p_\theta(x)$ is defined per prompt. "Success rate" on a dataset means the average of $p_\theta(x)$ over its prompts, which is the expected pass@1. Two policies with the same average can be very different: one at $p=0.5$ on every prompt, another at $p=1$ on half the prompts and $p=0$ on the rest. Section 7 shows that the second kind is exactly what failed training produces, which is why we log per-prompt $p$, not only the average.

3.3 Objective and policy gradient

The objective

Without regularization, training maximizes the average success probability over the training prompts:

$$J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_x\big[p_\theta(x)\big] = \mathbb{E}_x\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[r(x,y)\big].$$

With the KL penalty of Section 3.5 it becomes $J(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_x\big[p_\theta(x)-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}_x\big]$. The difficulty is that $\theta$ appears in the distribution we sample from, and sampling a discrete token cannot be differentiated. So we need an expression for $\nabla J$ that can be estimated from samples.

The score-function identity

For one prompt, write the expectation as a sum over responses and use $\nabla\pi = \pi\,\nabla\log\pi$:

$$\nabla_\theta\, p_\theta(x) = \sum_y r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \sum_y \pi_\theta(y\mid x)\, r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x) = \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\big[r(x,y)\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big].$$

The vector $\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)$ is the score of response $y$: the direction in parameter space that makes $y$ more likely. Averaging $r(y_i)\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_i)$ over sampled responses gives an unbiased estimate of $\nabla p$. This estimator is REINFORCE.

What the score looks like in our settings

  • Bandit. With logits $z\in\mathbb{R}^K$ and $\log\pi(y)=z_y-\log\sum_j e^{z_j}$, the score with respect to the logits is $e_y-\pi$: raise the sampled answer's logit, lower all logits in proportion to their probability. Hence $\partial p/\partial z_{y^\star}=p(1-p)$ and $\partial p/\partial z_j=-p\,\pi_j$ for wrong answers $j$.
  • Synthetic task. $\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\sum_\ell\log\pi_\theta(y_\ell\mid x)$, so the score is a sum of $L$ per-position terms. With respect to position $\ell$'s logits each term is $e_{y_\ell}-\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)_\ell$, and backpropagation carries it into the shared network weights. Because of the sum, the score grows with $L$, which is one reason step size and length interact.

Why REINFORCE alone is a poor estimator

With $r\in\{0,1\}$, only correct samples contribute: wrong samples are multiplied by $0$ and teach nothing. When $p$ is small almost every sample is wrong, so the estimate is usually zero and occasionally large, which means very high variance.

Definition

A baseline $b$ is a number subtracted from the reward. Because $\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}[\nabla\log\pi(y)]=\nabla\sum_y\pi(y)=\nabla 1=0$, subtracting any $b$ that does not depend on the response being scored leaves the expected gradient unchanged while reducing its variance. The result, possibly rescaled, is the advantage $A=r-b$. A positive advantage pushes the response's probability up, a negative one pushes it down, so now wrong samples also teach: they push their own probability down. Group-based rules (Section 3.4) build $b$ from the other responses to the same prompt.

Correct and incorrect responses separately

Splitting the expectation by reward gives two useful facts: $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r=1]=\nabla p/p$ and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r=0]=-\nabla p/(1-p)$. Their difference is Suk & Duan's gradient gap, and it equals $\nabla p/\big(p(1-p)\big)$. These two facts are all that is needed for the identity in Section 6.1.

From math to code

PyTorch minimizes a loss, so the trainer writes the update as a loss whose gradient is $-\widehat g$:

policy_loss = -(advantages * log_probs).mean()   # mean over B prompts and G responses
loss = policy_loss + beta * kl.mean()            # exact KL per prompt, averaged over prompts
loss.backward(); optimizer.step()

The advantages are computed from the rewards and detached, so gradients flow only through log_probs, which is exactly the score-function estimator. With plain SGD, optimizer.step() is $\theta\leftarrow\theta+\eta\,\widehat g$.

3.4 Groups, batches and advantage rules

Each training step draws $B$ prompts and samples a group of $G$ responses for each. With group mean $\bar r$, the update is

$$\theta \leftarrow \theta + \eta\,\widehat g,\qquad \widehat g = \frac{1}{B}\sum_{x}\widehat g_x,\qquad \widehat g_x = \frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} A_i\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x) \;-\; \beta\,\nabla_\theta\mathrm{KL}_x .$$

This is what "SGD" means in this project: one stochastic gradient step on the policy parameters $\theta$, with a fresh on-policy sample at every step. The step size $\eta$ is the learning rate of that step.

One training step, concretely

  1. Draw $B$ prompts from the training set (with replacement, in the current trainer).
  2. For each prompt, sample $G$ responses from the current policy. This is what on-policy means: the data always comes from the policy being updated, never from an older copy.
  3. Score each response with the verifier: $B\times G$ rewards.
  4. Compute the advantages within each group (the rules below).
  5. Take one gradient step, then throw the samples away.

Each step uses $N=B\,G$ samples. Comparing configurations at the same number of samples rather than the same number of steps matters whenever $G$ or $B$ changes; the trainer logs responses_sampled for this.

The advantage rules

They differ only in $A_i$:

Properties worth knowing

  • Zero sum. For mean-only, RLOO and GRPO, the advantages in a group add up to zero. In the bandit this cancels the "$-\pi$" part of every score $e_y-\pi$, so an update only touches the logits of answers that were actually sampled. Pass@$k$ shaping does not have this property.
  • Mean-only's small bias. Because $\bar r$ includes $r_i$ itself, the baseline depends on the response being scored. The effect is only a shrinking of the expected gradient by $(G-1)/G$, not a change of direction. RLOO removes it.
  • What std normalization does. Dividing by $\sqrt{\bar r(1-\bar r)}$ gives every informative group the same advantage scale, whatever its success rate. Prompts with $p$ near 0 or 1 have a small std, so they get up-weighted. Dr. GRPO argues this is a bias; Section 6.4 shows it is equivalent to optimizing a different objective. The $\varepsilon=10^{-5}$ only prevents division by zero in all-equal groups, whose advantages are zero anyway.
  • Pass@$k$ shaping. In main.py, with $n_{\text{wrong}}$ wrong responses in the group, a correct response's GRPO advantage is multiplied by $\binom{n_{\text{wrong}}}{k-1}/\binom{G-1}{k-1}$ (the chance that $k-1$ other draws from the group are all wrong) and a wrong one's by $\binom{n_{\text{wrong}}-1}{k-1}/\binom{G-1}{k-1}$. Correct answers on prompts that are already solved often get little credit.
  • What we leave out. PPO-style clipping and importance ratios only matter when the same samples are reused for several updates. We take exactly one step per batch, so they would have no effect, and we drop them. Per-token length normalization is also left out; our log-probabilities are summed over the $L$ tokens.
Definition

A group is informative if its rewards are not all equal; otherwise all advantages are zero and the prompt contributes nothing to the step. The probability is $\varphi(p,G) = 1-p^G-(1-p)^G$.

3.5 Reference policy and KL

Definition

The reference policy $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is a frozen copy of the policy at the start of RL. The penalty $\beta\,\mathrm{KL}$ keeps the policy near it. Reverse KL $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ is mode-seeking and is the standard choice; forward KL $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\|\pi_\theta)$ is mass-covering: it becomes very large when $\pi_\theta$ drops an answer that $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ supports. In our task both are computed exactly.

The formulas

$$\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}) = \sum_y \pi(y)\log\frac{\pi(y)}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)},\qquad \mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\,\|\,\pi) = \sum_y \pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\log\frac{\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)}{\pi(y)}.$$

Both are zero when $\pi=\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and positive otherwise. Because our policy is a product over positions, the KL of the whole sequence is the sum of the KLs at each position, so it costs $L\times K$ operations instead of a sum over all $K^L$ sequences. Policy.kl_divergence computes it that way, in either direction.

Mode-seeking vs. mass-covering, with numbers

Take $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}=(0.5,0.5)$ over two answers.

$(0.9, 0.1)$0.370.51

Reverse KL lets the policy drop an answer entirely at a finite cost; forward KL forbids it. That is why we expect forward KL to protect against prompts collapsing onto a single (wrong) answer (H4).

The KL-regularized optimum

If we could optimize $\mathbb{E}_\pi[A(y)]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})$ exactly over all distributions, the answer would be the reference reweighted by the advantage:

$$\pi^\star(y)\;\propto\;\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(y)\,\exp\!\big(A(y)/\beta\big).$$

With $A=r$ this multiplies the odds of success by $e^{1/\beta}$: $\operatorname{logit}p^\star=\operatorname{logit}p_{\mathrm{ref}}+1/\beta$. For $p_{\mathrm{ref}}=0.05$ and $\beta=2$ that is $p^\star=0.080$. Mroueh's fixed point (next section) is this formula with GRPO's weights in place of $A=r$. Smaller $\beta$ lets the policy move further; $\beta\to0$ removes the anchor.

The gradients

In the bandit, with respect to the logits: reverse KL has gradient $\pi\odot\big(\log(\pi/\pi_{\mathrm{ref}})-\mathrm{KL}\big)$ (used in main.py), and forward KL has the simpler $\pi-\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. The forward gradient keeps pulling with a fixed strength towards every answer the reference supports, however small $\pi$ gets.

In practice

  • Large-scale implementations estimate the KL from the sampled responses (for example the "$k_3$" estimator used in DeepSeek's GRPO), which adds noise. Zhang et al. (2025) show $k_3$ corresponds to an unnormalized KL. We avoid that issue entirely by computing the KL exactly.
  • In our code the reference is frozen when the Trainer is created, so any warm-start must happen before that. The reference then equals the base policy.

3.6 The "exact inner problem"

Definition

Mroueh's analysis does not take gradient steps. At iteration $n$ it fixes the advantage weights computed from $\pi_{n-1}$ and jumps to the policy that exactly maximizes the resulting KL-regularized objective. We replace that jump by one stochastic gradient step, which is what practice does.

The iteration, written out

With $p=p_{n-1}$, GRPO's population advantages are $a_+=\sqrt{(1-p)/p}$ for a correct response and $a_-=-\sqrt{p/(1-p)}$ for a wrong one. The inner problem is

$$\pi_n=\arg\max_\pi\;\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[a_+\,r(y)+a_-\,(1-r(y))\big]-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}),$$

and by the optimum of Section 3.5 its solution is $\pi_n\propto\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}\,e^{a(y)/\beta}$. Since $a_+-a_-=1/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$, the success probability updates as

$$\operatorname{logit}p_n=\operatorname{logit}p_{\mathrm{ref}}+\frac{1}{\beta\sqrt{p_{n-1}(1-p_{n-1})}}\quad\big(=: h(p_{n-1})\big).$$

Example with $p_{\mathrm{ref}}=0.05$, $\beta=2$, starting from $p_0=p_{\mathrm{ref}}$:

$0.050 \to 0.343 \to 0.131 \to 0.188 \to 0.159 \to 0.171 \to 0.166 \to 0.168 \to 0.167$

The first step overshoots, then it oscillates into the fixed point $p^\star\approx0.167$. The oscillation is the non-monotone $h$ at work.

4The setting: one synthetic task family

Definition: the task

A prompt is a feature vector $x\in\mathbb{R}^d$. A response is a sequence of $L$ tokens $y=(y_1,\dots,y_L)$, each from a vocabulary of size $K$. A fixed hidden linear rule picks the correct token at each position, $y^\star_\ell(x)=\arg\max_k\langle w_{\ell k},x\rangle$. The reward is 1 only if every token is correct.

Definition: the policy

A network maps $x$ to $L$ rows of $K$ logits, and tokens are drawn independently given $x$: $$\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\pi_\theta(y_\ell\mid x),\qquad p_\theta(x)=\prod_{\ell=1}^{L}\pi_\theta\big(y^\star_\ell(x)\mid x\big).$$ There is no autoregressive structure. Each prompt is a one-shot decision among $K^L$ possible sequences: a contextual bandit with a structured action space.

4.1 Two structural knobs

  • Length $L$ (Suk & Duan call it $T$). The score $\nabla\log\pi$ is a sum of $L$ terms. Together with $K$, it sets the sparsity: a uniform policy has $p=K^{-L}$.
  • Parameter sharing. Tabular: each prompt has its own free logits (equivalently, a linear layer on a one-hot prompt index), so an update on one prompt never touches another. Shared: one MLP for all prompts, so every update moves every prompt. Sharing can be graded by the network width or by how correlated prompt features are.
The bandit is a special case

The bandit in main.py is this family with $L=1$ and tabular parameters. So we do not need two settings. We run everything in the synthetic family and treat the bandit as one corner of it, which also serves as a correctness check, since its fixed points are known in closed form.

4.2 Setting $p_0$: the base policy

A freshly initialized policy is uniform, so $p_0=K^{-L}$ is tied to sparsity. To set $p_0$ independently, we pretrain briefly with supervised learning on the correct answers (a warm-start) and stop when the average success rate reaches the target. The warm-started network is the base policy, and its frozen copy is $\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$. A side effect is useful: the warm-start leaves some prompts easier than others, which gives the spread of difficulties that H2 needs. In the tabular case, $p_0$ can be set directly through the logits, as --p0 already does.

5The papers

5.1 Mroueh (2025): success amplification

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards: GRPO's effective loss, dynamics, and success amplification. arXiv:2503.06639.

Problem
What does GRPO with binary rewards optimize, and where does iterating it lead?
Result
GRPO's loss is a weighted contrastive loss between correct and incorrect samples: with std normalization, correct samples get weight $\sqrt{(1-p)/p}$ and incorrect ones $-\sqrt{p/(1-p)}$. If each iteration solves the KL-regularized problem exactly, success follows $p_n=h(p_{n-1})$, whose fixed points satisfy $\operatorname{logit}p^\star=\operatorname{logit}p_{\mathrm{ref}}+\Omega(p^\star)/\beta$ with $\Omega=1/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$. So $p^\star>p_{\mathrm{ref}}$: success is amplified.
Assumptions
Reverse KL, no clipping, exact expectations ($G\to\infty$), exact inner problem, per-prompt analysis.
In our framework
$\Omega$ is the $G\to\infty$ limit of $W_G$. Finite $G$, SGD and shared parameters each change the answer (H4).

5.2 Suk & Duan (2025): gradient gap and step-size thresholds

On the optimization dynamics of RLVR: Gradient gap and step size thresholds. arXiv:2510.08539.

Problem
When does RLVR converge, and how large can the step size be?
Result
The gradient gap is the difference between the expected score of correct and incorrect responses. The paper proves a sufficient step-size condition for convergence and lower bounds where a constant step makes performance collapse, with the threshold scaling like $1/T$ in the response length and depending on the success rate through $(1-p)$. Progress follows a logistic law in the cumulative alignment with the gap.
Assumptions
Exact (population) gradients in the main analysis.
In our framework
The gradient gap is $\nabla p/(p(1-p))$, so their update direction is ours up to the scalar $W$. We test the threshold with stochastic groups and shared parameters, and propose the mechanism behind the collapse (H3).

To pin down in Ankush's review: the exact form of the $p$-dependence (does the threshold grow or shrink with $p$?) and what the paper says about length normalization.

5.3 Thrampoulidis, Mahdavi & Deng (2026): advantage shaping as surrogate rewards

Advantage shaping as surrogate reward maximization: Unifying pass@K policy gradients. TMLR 2026, arXiv:2510.23049.

Problem
Many papers reshape GRPO's advantages. What does each reshaping optimize?
Result
Shaping the advantage corresponds to maximizing a surrogate reward $F(p)$. For pass@$k$, $F=1-(1-p)^k$, with gradient $k(1-p)^{k-1}\nabla p$. They derive an unbiased leave-one-out estimator ($\mathrm{RLOO}_k$) and a biased std-normalized one ($\mathrm{GRPO}_k$).
Assumptions
One update per fresh group, no clipping, no KL; the surrogate view is a population statement, with finite-sample effects left open.
In our framework
$F' = W$ exactly. Computing $W_G$ at finite $G$ gives the surrogate that a finite-group rule actually optimizes (H2).

6The unifying framework

6.1 The advantage-gap identity

Fix a prompt. Responses in a group are independent, and $A_i$ depends only on the group's rewards. Condition on whether $y_i$ is correct:

$$\mathbb{E}\big[A_i\nabla\log\pi(y_i)\big] = p\,\mathbb{E}[A_i\mid r_i{=}1]\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}1] + (1-p)\,\mathbb{E}[A_i\mid r_i{=}0]\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}0].$$

Since $\mathbb{E}[r\nabla\log\pi]=\nabla p$ and $\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi]=0$, we have $p\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}1]=\nabla p$ and $(1-p)\,\mathbb{E}[\nabla\log\pi\mid r{=}0]=-\nabla p$. Therefore

$$\boxed{\;\mathbb{E}\big[\widehat g_x\big] = W_G(p_x)\,\nabla p_x,\qquad W_G(p)=\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0].\;}$$

Nothing here depends on the parameterization, on $L$, or on how many correct answers exist. The assumptions are binary rewards, independent samples, rewards-only advantages, and a summed log-probability. Per-token averaging with variable lengths breaks it.

Verified

On a shared MLP with $L=2$, $K=3$, $G=4$ and $p=0.109$, the average of 40,000 sampled group gradients matches $W_G\nabla p$ with cosine similarity $1.0000$ and norm ratio $1.01$ (Monte Carlo error), for mean-only ($W=0.75$), RLOO ($W=1$) and GRPO ($W=1.687$).

6.3 Exact equivalences: the grid collapses

  • RLOO is mean-only with a larger step: $A^{\mathrm{RLOO}}_i=\frac{G}{G-1}(r_i-\bar r)$ for every sample, not just on average. So RLOO at step $\eta$ is mean-only at $\eta\,G/(G-1)$, sample path by sample path.
  • At $G=2$, GRPO and RLOO are the same rule: both give $\pm1$ in a mixed group and $0$ otherwise (up to GRPO's tiny $\varepsilon$), so $W_2\equiv1$ for GRPO at every $p$ (verified numerically), and both equal mean-only at $2\eta$.
  • For a single prompt, only the scale of $W$ matters, and scale is a step size. Differences between rules in expectation come only from how $W$ varies across prompts with different $p$, or over time as $p$ changes. Their noise can differ even when their expectations match.

So the advantage-rule axis is really "which shape of $W$", and the rule $\times$ $G$ $\times$ $\eta$ grid has far fewer distinct points than it seems.

6.4 The three papers as three readings of $W$

  • What is optimized (Thrampoulidis et al.). The expected update is gradient ascent on $\sum_x F_G(p_x)$ with $F_G' = W_G$. At $G=2$, GRPO's surrogate is $F=p$: plain pass@1, like mean-only. As $G\to\infty$ it becomes $2\arcsin\sqrt p$ (Davis & Recht). Group size changes the objective, not only the noise.
  • Where it stops (Mroueh). With a KL penalty, the expected update vanishes where $W_G$ balances the KL pull. In the tabular case this gives $\operatorname{logit}p^\star=\operatorname{logit}p_{\mathrm{ref}}+W_G(p^\star)/\beta$ for reverse KL, and $\frac{p^\star-p_{\mathrm{ref}}}{p^\star(1-p^\star)}=W_G(p^\star)/\beta$ for forward KL. Both were checked against an exact-gradient simulation (numbers in H4). With shared parameters the balance is a sum over all prompts, so per-prompt fixed points are coupled.
  • Whether it is stable (Suk & Duan). Prompt $x$'s own contribution to its log-odds $z_x = \operatorname{logit}p_x$ in one step is, to first order, $$\Delta z_x \approx s_x\cdot\big\|\nabla_\theta z_x\big\|^2,\qquad s_x = \frac{\eta}{B}\,W_G(p_x)\,p_x(1-p_x).$$ Every knob that the theory treats separately (rule, $G$, $B$, $\eta$) enters this effective step through one product.

6.5 The missing piece: interference and absorbing states

The identity is about expectations. Two facts about fluctuations explain how training fails.

  1. Tabular training cannot fail. With $\beta=0$ and a zero-sum rule (mean-only, RLOO, GRPO), a tabular update raises the correct logit and lowers only sampled wrong logits. So $p_x$ never decreases, at any step size. Any collapse therefore needs other prompts to push $p_x$ down, which needs shared parameters. The cross term is $$\Delta z_x \;\ni\; \frac{\eta}{B}\sum_{x'\neq x}\Big(\tfrac1G\textstyle\sum_i A_i^{(x')}\,\langle\nabla z_x,\nabla\log\pi(y_i^{(x')})\rangle\Big),$$ which has random sign and grows with $\eta$ and with the overlap between prompts' gradients.
  2. $p\approx0$ is absorbing. If a prompt is pushed to a near-deterministic wrong answer, then $\varphi\approx0$: no group contains a correct answer, its own term vanishes, and only interference can move it. The probability of escaping in one step is $1-(1-p)^G$, so $G$ controls escape. Similarly $p\approx1$ is absorbing, but harmlessly.
The resulting picture

Each prompt's log-odds performs a random walk: an upward drift of size $s_x$ from its own samples, noise from other prompts through the shared parameters, and two absorbing ends. The final fraction of prompts solved is the probability of reaching the top before the bottom. Large steps raise the noise faster than the drift, so more prompts are absorbed at the bottom; this is the step-size threshold. The KL penalty pulls away from both ends, so it should raise the threshold, and forward KL, which strongly penalizes dropping reference-supported answers, should protect better than reverse KL.

8Hypotheses and experiments

Each hypothesis is a consequence of Section 6. Each test is a collapse plot: if the framework is right, runs with very different raw hyperparameters fall on one curve when plotted against the predicted effective quantity. Unless stated, all runs use plain SGD (Adam rescales steps per coordinate and would hide the effects), at least 3 seeds, and exact evaluation.

H1. The advantage-gap identity and its equivalences nearly done

Hypothesis

The mean update equals $W_G(p)\nabla p$ in every configuration, and the exact equivalences of Section 6.3 hold along whole training runs.

Test
(a) Sampled vs. predicted mean gradient across $L$, sharing, rule, $G$; with small $K^L$ the true $\nabla p$ is exact by autograd. (b) Train RLOO at $\eta$ and mean-only at $\eta G/(G-1)$ with the same seed: the trajectories should be identical. Same for GRPO and RLOO at $G=2$.
Why first
It is the foundation; it also catches implementation bugs.
Refuted if
Any systematic mismatch beyond Monte Carlo error.

H2. Group size changes what GRPO optimizes needs warm-start

Hypothesis

With prompts of mixed difficulty, each rule redistributes progress across prompts in proportion to its $W_G(p)$. GRPO at small $G$ behaves like pass@1 ascent; at large $G$ it increasingly favours prompts near $p=0$ and $p=1$; pass@$k$ shaping favours hard prompts.

Setting
Warm-started shared MLP with a spread of per-prompt $p$; small $\eta$ (drift-dominated); $\beta=0$.
Vary
Rule (mean-only, GRPO, $\mathrm{RLOO}_k$, $\mathrm{GRPO}_k$); $G\in\{2,4,16,64\}$; $k\in\{2,4\}$.
Measure
Per-prompt change in $p$ over a window, binned by starting $p$, divided by mean-only's.
Collapse plot
That ratio against $W_G(p)/W^{\text{mean}}_G$: all rules and $G$ on one line.
Refuted if
The per-bin ratios do not follow $W_G$'s shape, for example because interference redistributes progress more than $W$ does.

H3. Stability is set by one effective step and by sharing main study

Hypothesis

(a) The outcome of training (final success, absorbed fraction) depends on $\eta$, $B$, $G$ and the rule mainly through the effective step $\bar s = \frac{\eta}{B}\,\overline{W_G(p)\,p(1-p)}$ at the start of training. (b) The threshold exists only with sharing: tabular runs never collapse, and the threshold in $\bar s$ drops as the overlap between prompts' gradients grows. (c) The dependence on $L$ and $p_0$ enters through $\|\nabla z\|^2$ and the overlap, both measurable at initialization.

Setting
Synthetic family, $\beta=0$.
Vary
$\eta$ on a log grid; $B$ and $G$ (including fixed-budget pairs); rule; $L\in\{1,2,4\}$; sharing: tabular, MLP widths $\{16,64,256\}$, and correlated vs. independent prompt features.
Measure
Final success, absorbed fraction, entropy; at initialization, $\|\nabla z_x\|^2$ and the mean cosine between prompts' gradients (the interference).

Tabular runs collapse, or configurations with equal $\bar s$ and interference end in clearly different places.

H4. KL sets where training stops and how safely it gets there tabular ready

Hypothesis

(a) With small steps, training settles at the finite-$G$ balance point $\operatorname{logit}p^\star=\operatorname{logit}p_{\mathrm{ref}}+W_G(p^\star)/\beta$ (reverse KL) or $\frac{p^\star-p_{\mathrm{ref}}}{p^\star(1-p^\star)}=W_G(p^\star)/\beta$ (forward KL), not Mroueh's $G\to\infty$ value. (b) With larger steps, $p$ fluctuates around $p^\star$ with a variance proportional to $\eta$ and decreasing in $\beta$. (c) Because KL pulls away from the absorbing ends, the step-size threshold of H3 rises with $\beta$, and more so for forward KL.

Predictions
Example, $p_{\mathrm{ref}}=0.05$, $\beta=2$, GRPO, reverse KL: $p^\star=0.109$ at $G=4$, $0.157$ at $G=16$, about $0.17$ as $G\to\infty$. Mean-only, $G\to\infty$: $p^\star=0.0798$ for reverse KL and $0.0916$ for forward KL, both matching an exact-gradient simulation. Earlier bandit runs at $\beta=2$ landed within about $0.01$ of the finite-$G$ values.

H5. Group size, budget and escape (separate study) pilot exists

Hypothesis

Under a fixed sample budget $N=BG$, group size trades three effects: (i) better within-prompt signal (large for GRPO, negligible for mean-only, Section 7.3), (ii) a larger effective step per prompt ($s\propto G/N$), and (iii) a higher chance of escaping $p\approx0$, $1-(1-p)^G$. So the best $G$ grows as $p_0$ falls (to make groups informative, $G\gtrsim1/p_0$), is larger for GRPO than for mean-only, and the best $\eta$ falls as $G$ grows.

Setting
Tabular first (no interference, so (ii) has no downside), then shared.
Vary
$p_0\in\{0.01,0.03,0.1,0.3\}$; $G\in\{2,\dots,64\}$ with $N$ fixed; rule; $\eta$ tuned per configuration.
Measure
Samples to reach $p=0.5$; informative fraction against $\varphi$; with mixed difficulty, the excess of uninformative groups over $\varphi(\bar p)$ (Nie et al.).
Collapse plot
Samples-to-target against $G\,p_0$, compared with Bay & Yearick's $G\,p(1-p)$ law.
Refuted if
The best $G$ does not move with $p_0$, or mean-only and GRPO prefer the same $G$.

How the hypotheses connect

  • H1 establishes the equation. H2, H3 and H4 test its three readings: what is optimized, whether it is stable, and where it stops. H5 tests the noise side that the equation leaves out.
  • The links are explicit: $G$ enters H2 (through the shape of $W$), H3 (through $s$), H4 (through $p^\star$) and H5 (through noise and escape); $\beta$ enters H4 and modifies H3's threshold; sharing is what makes H3 possible at all.
  • The bandit is the tabular, $L=1$ corner of every experiment, where most predictions are exact.

10What we could conclude

Possible statements for the final report, depending on the results:

  • If H1 and H2 hold: the choice of advantage rule and group size is a choice of surrogate objective, given exactly by $F_G=\int W_G$. GRPO with small groups is pass@1 optimization; with large groups it is closer to $\arcsin\sqrt p$.
  • If H3 holds: instability in RLVR is prompts being absorbed into confident wrong answers through interference, and it is controlled by one effective step size, not by $\eta$, $G$, $B$ and the rule separately. Tuning reduces to one number plus a measure of interference.
  • If H4 holds: the KL penalty has a second role beyond setting $p^\star$: it guards against absorption, and its direction matters for stability.
  • If H5 holds: the right group size depends on the rule: with mean-only it barely matters per sample, with GRPO it should grow as $p_0$ falls.
  • If a hypothesis fails: the collapse plot shows where, which is itself the finding, for example that interference matters more than $W$ for H2.

$K$, $L$ ($=T$), $d$Vocabulary size, response length, prompt feature dimension
$G$, $B$, $N=BG$Group size, prompts per step, samples per step
$k$Attempts in pass@$k$ (not $G$)
$A_i$Advantage of response $i$
$W_G(p)$Advantage gap $\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}1]-\mathbb{E}[A\mid r{=}0]$; $\Omega$ in Mroueh's notation as $G\to\infty$
$F_G$Surrogate objective, $F_G'=W_G$
$s_x$Effective step on prompt $x$: $\frac{\eta}{B}W_G(p_x)\,p_x(1-p_x)$
$\beta$, $\eta$KL strength, step size
$\varphi$Probability a group is informative, $1-p^G-(1-p)^G$
