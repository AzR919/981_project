\begin{abstract}
  Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR), and in particular
  Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO), is now the standard recipe for
  training reasoning in large language models. Recent theory makes concrete
  predictions about its dynamics: how many sampled groups carry a learning
  signal, whether GRPO amplifies the success probability, and when a step
  size is too large. These predictions are derived for exact gradients and
  idealized policies, and are hard to check at LLM scale, where every run is
  expensive and the true success probability is unknown. We propose an
  experiment-centred project that tests them on a small synthetic task with
  a binary verifier. Its difficulty is set exactly by the answer length, and
  the policy's success probability can be computed in closed form, so runs
  take seconds on a CPU. We vary the initial success rate, the number of
  sampled answers per prompt, reward sparsity, KL strength, and step size,
  and compare each result with the corresponding theoretical prediction. As
  a small theoretical contribution, we extend the success-amplification
  fixed point of \citet{mroueh2025rlvr} from infinite to finite group size,
  which gives closed forms for mean-only and leave-one-out baselines and a
  testable prediction of how the group size shifts GRPO's fixed point. We
  also show that on-policy SGD shares these fixed points with Mroueh's
  exact iteration but not their stability. We close with a confirmation on
  a 0.5B-parameter LLM.
\end{abstract}

\section{Motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

DeepSeek-R1~\citep{guo2025deepseekr1} showed that a language model can learn
long chain-of-thought reasoning from nothing more than a binary ``is the
final answer correct?'' reward, optimized with
GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. Since then, practitioners have relied on
rules of thumb: sample more answers per prompt for hard problems, normalize
advantages by the group standard deviation, average the loss over tokens,
and filter out prompts that are too easy or too hard. Each of these choices
changes the optimization problem, and it is not clear which ones matter or
why.

A first wave of theory now gives precise answers in simplified
settings~\citep{mroueh2025rlvr,suk2025gradientgap,thrampoulidis2026passk,bay2026grpostd}.
The theory has two practical gaps. First, its assumptions (exact
expectations, one prompt, tabular or log-linear policies, an exactly solved
inner problem) are far from the stochastic, finite-sample regime in which
GRPO is actually run. Second, LLM experiments cannot isolate a single
variable, because the success rate, task difficulty, and response length
all change together. Notably, neither \citet{mroueh2025rlvr} nor
\citet{suk2025gradientgap} sweeps the step size or the KL strength in its
experiments.

We aim to close this gap with controlled experiments. On a task where every
quantity is known exactly, we can change one knob at a time and see whether
the theory's predictions survive finite samples, a learned function
approximator, and stochastic gradients. The results tell practitioners when
to spend compute on more samples per prompt, when a reward is too sparse to
learn from, and which loss normalizations make training stable.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{GRPO and RLVR.}
GRPO~\citep{shao2024deepseekmath} drops PPO's value network. For each prompt
it samples a group of $G$ answers and uses the group-normalized reward
$(r_i - \bar r)/\mathrm{std}(r)$ as the advantage.
DeepSeek-R1~\citep{guo2025deepseekr1} scaled this up with rule-based
verifiers (an accuracy reward plus a format reward), $G=16$, and a small KL
coefficient. Dr.~GRPO~\citep{liu2025drgrpo} argues that the std-normalization
and the per-response length normalization introduce biases, and
RLOO~\citep{ahmadian2024rloo} uses a leave-one-out baseline instead. We use
all three as advantage variants.

\paragraph{Success amplification~\citep{mroueh2025rlvr}.}
For binary rewards with a reverse-KL penalty and no clipping, the paper
shows that GRPO's objective is a contrastive loss between correct and
incorrect samples from the previous policy. If each iteration solves the
KL-regularized problem exactly, the success probability follows a recurrence
$p_n = h(p_{n-1})$, and every fixed point satisfies $p^\ast > \pref$, so
GRPO amplifies success. For mean-only normalization the fixed point is
explicit; for mean+std normalization it has no closed form and need not be
unique. A local-convergence condition gives a lower bound on $\beta$, and
the paper reports divergent iterates at $\beta = 5$ with $\pref = 0.001$.
We test whether SGD training reaches the predicted $p^\ast$ across KL
strengths, extend the prediction to finite $G$, and ask whether SGD
inherits the iteration's instability (\cref{sec:finiteG}).
\citet{davis2025objective} give a complementary view: with binary rewards,
several RL algorithms are stochastic gradient ascent on a monotone
transform of the success probability, and for GRPO the transform is
$\arcsin\sqrt{p}$. \citet{rad2026rateorfate} analyse GRPO on a bandit as a
replicator-style flow and show a phase transition under verifier noise;
theirs is the closest analysis in style to ours, but it has no KL penalty.
\citet{ding2026klmisfires} list ways the KL term misbehaves in group
policy optimization, including KL updates on groups whose rewards are all
equal.

\paragraph{Gradient gap and step-size thresholds~\citep{suk2025gradientgap}.}
The paper defines the gradient gap, the difference between the expected
score of correct and incorrect responses, and proves a sufficient step-size
condition for convergence together with lower-bound constructions
where a constant step makes performance decrease to zero. With exact
gradients, the threshold scales as $1/T$ in the response length $T$ and
shrinks with the success rate through $(1-J)$; the paper argues that GRPO's
$1/|y|$ normalization provides exactly this scaling and Dr.~GRPO's does not.
Its experiments use a single step size. We test whether a sharp transition
appears in a stochastic, learned-policy setting, and how it moves with
response length when responses of different lengths share one learning
rate. Two recent results sharpen this question. \citet{ge2026normalization}
show that std-normalization acts as an adaptive step size matched to local
curvature, with a speed-up that depends on how much the per-prompt reward
variance differs across prompts. \citet{ding2026lengthimpossibility} prove
that with outcome rewards no length weighting is both unbiased and
length-invariant; GRPO and Dr.~GRPO are the two ends of the family
$f_\alpha(L) = L^{\alpha - 1}$, $\alpha \in [0, 1]$.

\paragraph{Group size and informative prompts.}
A group with all-equal rewards has zero advantage. DAPO's dynamic
sampling~\citep{yu2025dapo} and selective rollouts~\citep{zheng2025greso}
discard such groups, and \citet{foster2025learnability} sample prompts in
proportion to $p(1-p)$. \citet{bay2026grpostd} show that GRPO, Dr.~GRPO and
DAPO differ only in how they use the group standard deviation, derive a
closed-form group-size law in $G\,p(1-p)$, and check it on a Bernoulli toy
model at $G=8$ and by subsampling logged rollouts.
\citet{ghosh2026predictable} model the mean reward trajectory as a damped
oscillator and predict that it is nearly independent of $G$ to leading
order. \citet{zhou2026ustat} show that the GRPO gradient is a U-statistic,
derive its mean squared error, and propose a scaling law for the group
size. \citet{nie2026starvation} prove that when the success rate varies
across prompts, the fraction of all-equal groups exceeds the prediction
from the average success rate, and that a fixed-reference advantage
$A = 2r - 1$ outperforms Dr.~GRPO at $G = 4$ on GSM8K. Empirical results
pull in two directions: \citet{hu2025brorl} revive stalled training by
scaling to hundreds of rollouts per prompt, while \citet{wu2025ittakestwo}
retain 97.6\% of the performance of $G = 16$ with $G = 2$.
\citet{plyusov2026fgrpo} show that a group can have mixed rewards yet miss
rare correct solutions, with a non-monotonic dependence on $G$. Prompt
selection methods choose prompts by predicted learnability: ThinkPrior
starts from an offline difficulty prior~\citep{sha2026thinkprior}, and
ARCUS tracks difficulty in Fisher--Rao coordinates of the pass
rate~\citep{okonkwo2026arcus}.

We do not claim the informative-group fraction (Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative})
or the $p(1-p)$ scaling as new. The closest prior work to ours is
\citet{bay2026grpostd}, \citet{zhou2026ustat}, \citet{nie2026starvation}
and \citet{plyusov2026fgrpo}, each of which varies $G$ in theory or in a
toy model. Our contribution is a controlled \emph{training} grid over $G$,
$p_0$, KL strength and step size, with every quantity measured exactly,
compared against these closed forms.

\paragraph{Pass@$K$ advantage shaping.}
\citet{walder2025passk} and \citet{chen2025passk} train directly for
pass@$K$. \citet{thrampoulidis2026passk} show that advantage-shaping
heuristics, such as hard-example upweighting, implicitly maximize surrogate
rewards such as pass@$K$, and derive an unbiased leave-one-out pass@$K$
advantage. We test whether optimizing pass@$K$ preserves response diversity
and at what cost to pass@1.

\paragraph{Entropy and diversity collapse.}
\citet{yue2025doesrl} show that RLVR-trained models beat their base model
at small $k$ but lose to it at large $k$. \citet{cui2025entropy} trace the
fall in entropy to the covariance between a token's log-probability and its
advantage, and fit an empirical law $R = -a e^{H} + b$ between performance
and entropy. \citet{yuan2026overtraining} attribute diversity collapse to
overtraining prompts that are already solved, and find that updating only
on prompts with zero observed successes raises pass@256 above the base
model. \citet{shao2025spurious} find that even random rewards improve some
models, which argues for a random-reward control in LLM experiments.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem}

\paragraph{A synthetic verifiable task.}
A prompt is a feature vector $x \in \R^d$. A response is a sequence
$y = (y_1, \dots, y_L)$ of $L$ tokens, each from a vocabulary of $K$
options. A hidden rule assigns each position a correct token
$c_t(x) = \argmax_k (W_t x)_k$. The verifier returns
\begin{equation}
  r(x, y) = \1\{\, y_t = c_t(x) \ \ \forall t = 1, \dots, L \,\},
\end{equation}
so reward is all-or-nothing, as with exact-match answers or unit tests. A
uniform policy succeeds with probability $p_0 = K^{-L}$, so the answer
length $L$ and vocabulary size $K$ set the reward sparsity exactly. To vary
the initial success rate without changing the task, we also warm-start the
policy with a short supervised phase that stops at a target $p_0$. This
plays the role of a base model. For the step-size experiment we also use a
\emph{mixed-length} variant in which each prompt has its own length
$L(x) \in \{2, \dots, 8\}$. For the diversity experiment we use a
\emph{multi-solution} variant in which $m$ of the $K$ tokens are correct at
each position, so a prompt has $m^L$ correct responses and diversity among
correct answers can be measured; the base model starts with unequal
probabilities on them, so that some correct responses are rare.

\paragraph{Policy.}
$\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{t=1}^{L} \pi_\theta(y_t \mid x, t)$, where
each factor is the softmax output of a small two-layer MLP. Because the
policy factorizes, the exact success probability on a prompt is
$p(x) = \prod_t \pi_\theta(c_t(x) \mid x, t)$, and
$\text{pass@}k = 1 - (1 - p(x))^k$. Our learning curves therefore have no
evaluation noise, and the KL to $\piref$ can be computed exactly rather than
estimated. The factors are conditionally independent given $(x,t)$, so the
policy is not autoregressive: a length-$L$ response is a product of $L$
coupled bandits sharing one network. With $L = 1$ the policy is a single
softmax, which matches the bandit setting used in the theory papers.

\paragraph{Objective and update.}
We maximize the KL-regularized expected reward
\begin{equation}
  J(\theta) = \E_x \Bigl[ \E_{y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)} r(x, y)
    - \beta \, \KL\bigl(\pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x) \,\|\, \piref(\cdot \mid x)\bigr) \Bigr].
\end{equation}
Each step samples $B$ prompts and $G$ responses per prompt, computes
advantages $A_i$ within each group, and takes one gradient step on
$-\frac{1}{BG} \sum A_i \, \ell_i(\theta) + \beta \, \KL$, where
$\ell_i = \log \pi_\theta(y_i \mid x)$ is the summed log-probability unless
stated otherwise. Training is fully on-policy, so PPO's ratio clipping is
inactive. We compare four advantages: \texttt{grpo}
$(r_i - \bar r)/\sqrt{\bar r(1-\bar r) + \epsilon}$; \texttt{mean-only}
$r_i - \bar r$ (Dr.~GRPO); \texttt{rloo}, which subtracts the leave-one-out
mean; and the unbiased pass@$k$ advantage of
\citet{thrampoulidis2026passk}, implemented in its combinatorial form to
avoid the $0/0$ case.

\paragraph{A first quantity to verify.}
If every response in a group gets the same reward, all advantages are zero
and the group gives no gradient. With per-prompt success $p$, the
probability that a group is informative is
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:informative}
  \phi(p, G) = 1 - p^G - (1 - p)^G \approx G p \quad (p \ll 1).
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:informative} links all three knobs: sparsity sets $p$,
the initial success rate is $p$ at step 0, and the group size is $G$.
Because our $p$ is exact, matching Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative} validates the
harness; the experiments ask how this per-step quantity translates into
learning speed over a run.

\subsection{Success amplification at finite group size}
\label{sec:finiteG}

\citet{mroueh2025rlvr} works in the limit where the group statistics equal
their population values. Practical runs use $G \in [2, 64]$, and at small
$p$ most groups are uninformative, so we extend the fixed point to finite
$G$. Fix a prompt with success probability $p$ under the current policy,
and let $r_2, \dots, r_G \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ be the other rewards in
a group. Define the expected advantage of a correct and of an incorrect
response,
\begin{equation}
  a^+_G(p) = \E[A_1 \mid r_1 = 1], \qquad
  a^-_G(p) = -\E[A_1 \mid r_1 = 0], \qquad
  \Omega_G(p) = a^+_G(p) + a^-_G(p).
\end{equation}
Both are finite sums over $j \sim \Bin(G-1, p)$, the number of other
correct responses. For \texttt{grpo},
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:omegaG}
  \Omega_G(p) = \sum_{j=0}^{G-1} \binom{G-1}{j} p^j (1-p)^{G-1-j}
  \left[ \frac{1 - \frac{j+1}{G}}{\sqrt{\frac{j+1}{G}\bigl(1-\frac{j+1}{G}\bigr) + \epsilon}}
  + \frac{\frac{j}{G}}{\sqrt{\frac{j}{G}\bigl(1-\frac{j}{G}\bigr) + \epsilon}} \right].
\end{equation}

\begin{proposition}
  \label{prop:finiteG}
  Let the policy be a softmax over all responses with independent logits
  for each prompt (e.g.\ a tabular policy with $L=1$), and let updates be
  on-policy with the exact KL penalty. Any stationary point of the expected
  update satisfies
  \begin{equation}
    \label{eq:fixedpoint}
    \logit p^\ast = \logit \pref + \frac{\Omega_G(p^\ast)}{\beta}.
  \end{equation}
  In particular, $\Omega_G \equiv (G-1)/G$ for \texttt{mean-only},
  $\Omega_G \equiv 1$ for \texttt{rloo}, and $\Omega_2 \approx 1$ for
  \texttt{grpo}, so these three fixed points are explicit. As
  $G \to \infty$, $\Omega_G(p) \to (p(1-p)+\epsilon)^{-1/2}$ and
  Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixedpoint} recovers the fixed-point equation
  $p^\ast = h(p^\ast)$ of \citet{mroueh2025rlvr}.
\end{proposition}

The proof (\cref{app:proof}) uses exchangeability of the group to write
the expected update as the gradient of a reward-tilted objective, whose
KL-regularized optimum is $\pi \propto \piref \exp(w(r)/\beta)$. The
stationary points of on-policy SGD are therefore exactly the fixed points
of Mroueh's exact iteration, with $\Omega_\infty$ replaced by $\Omega_G$.
A Monte Carlo check over $G \in \{2, 4, 16\}$ and
$p \in \{0.05, 0.3, 0.7\}$ agrees with Eq.~\eqref{eq:omegaG} and the
closed forms to within $0.01$.

The proposition also connects to the view of \citet{davis2025objective}.
For the same policy class, maximizing $F(p) - \beta\,\KL(\pi \,\|\, \piref)$
for a monotone $F$ gives the stationarity condition
$\logit p = \logit \pref + F'(p)/\beta$. Our fixed points are therefore
those of an effective objective $F_G$ with $F_G' = \Omega_G$: $F_G(p) =
\frac{G-1}{G}\,p$ for \texttt{mean-only}, $F_G(p) = p$ for \texttt{rloo},
and, for \texttt{grpo} as $G \to \infty$ with $\epsilon = 0$,
$F_\infty(p) = 2\arcsin\sqrt{p}$, whose derivative is
$1/\sqrt{p(1-p)}$. This matches Davis and Recht's transform. At the success
rates in \cref{tab:omega}, finite $G$ replaces it with a flatter one.

\paragraph{Same fixed points, different stability.}
Let $z = \logit p$. For the tabular policy with $L = 1$, the wrong
answers start with equal logits and stay equal, so the expected-gradient
flow reduces to a scalar equation (\cref{app:proof}):
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:flow}
  \dot z = \kappa(z) \Bigl[ \Omega_G(\sigma(z)) - \beta\,(z - \logit \pref) \Bigr],
  \qquad \kappa(z) > 0 .
\end{equation}
A fixed point is stable under this flow when its slope
$s = \Omega_G'(p^\ast)\,p^\ast(1-p^\ast)/\beta$ satisfies $s < 1$. Under
Mroueh's map it is stable only when $|s| < 1$. Starting from $\pref$, the
flow climbs to the smallest fixed point above $\pref$, which always has
$s < 1$ and is therefore stable. The exact map can instead overshoot it.
With $G = \infty$ and $\pref = 10^{-3}$, our numerical solution of the map
gives a 2-cycle for $\beta \in [3, 5]$, consistent with Mroueh's reported
divergence. For $\beta \in [0.5, 2]$ the map escapes to $p \approx 1$,
while the flow settles at $p^\ast \in [0.02, 0.17]$. With
$\pref = 1/8$, both converge to the same point for every $\beta$ in our
grid, so this effect only appears for sparse rewards.

\Cref{tab:omega} shows that the correction is large where it matters:
at $p = 0.05$ and $G = 16$, the finite-$G$ weight is $29\%$ below the
infinite-$G$ value, so the infinite-$G$ formula overstates how much
std-normalization amplifies sparse prompts. The proposition also makes the
\texttt{grpo} vs.\ \texttt{mean-only} comparison precise. At equal $\beta$,
\texttt{grpo}'s larger $\Omega_G$ acts largely as a smaller effective
$\beta$, so we compare the two both at equal $\beta$ and at matched
effective strength, to separate scale from shape. The proposition does not
cover the factorized policy with $L > 1$, which cannot represent a tilt on
the joint event ``all positions correct''. Measuring that gap is part of
E2.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Exact pass@1 and pass@$k$ on held-out prompts; the measured fraction of
informative groups, compared with the batch average
$\E_x[\phi(p(x), G)]$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative} and with
$\phi(\E_x[p(x)], G)$, which overestimates it when $p(x)$ varies across
prompts~\citep{nie2026starvation}; diversity, measured as policy entropy,
the number of distinct responses per group, and (in the multi-solution
variant) the mass on the rarest correct responses; the
log-probability--advantage covariance that drives entropy
change~\citep{cui2025entropy};
exact KL to $\piref$; gradient norm; and steps to reach a target success
rate. We report a run as \emph{stalled} if it never improves on $p_0$, and
as \emph{collapsed} if its final success falls below $p_0$. All results are
averaged over at least 3 seeds.

\section{Plan}
\label{sec:experiments}

\paragraph{Ambitious goal.}
Produce a map of when GRPO learns, stalls, or collapses as a function of
$(p_0, G, \text{sparsity}, \text{step size})$, with each region explained
by one of the theory papers, and confirm the main trends on a small LLM.

\paragraph{Core experiments (what we commit to).}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em, labelindent=0em]
  \item[E1] \textbf{Initial success rate $\times$ group size.} Sweep
    $L \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ ($p_0$ from $0.25$ to $10^{-3}$) and
    $G \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32\}$. We run two budgets: a fixed number of
    prompts per step, and a fixed number of samples per step ($B \times G$
    constant). Hypotheses: the measured informative-group fraction follows
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative} (a harness check); a larger $G$ helps mostly
    when $p_0$ is small; at a fixed sample budget, the advantage of larger
    $G$ shrinks; entropy and distinct responses fall as success rises. At
    a fixed sample budget we expect $G = 2$ to match large $G$ when $p_0$
    is moderate~\citep{wu2025ittakestwo} but to fall behind when $p_0$ is
    small, where very large groups are needed~\citep{hu2025brorl}; locating
    the crossover in $p_0$ reconciles the two results. We also test the
    prediction of \citet{ghosh2026predictable} that the mean trajectory is
    nearly invariant to $G$, and compare our measured learning speeds and
    gradient variances with the group-size laws of \citet{bay2026grpostd}
    and \citet{zhou2026ustat}. Finally, we repeat the grid with prompts
    whose $p_0$ is spread out rather than equal, where
    \citet{ge2026normalization} predict that std-normalization helps most
    and \citet{nie2026starvation} predict more all-equal groups than the
    average $p_0$ suggests.

  \item[E2] \textbf{Success amplification~\citep{mroueh2025rlvr}.} With
    $L = 1$, use $K = 8$ ($\pref = 1/8$) and $K = 1000$
    ($\pref = 10^{-3}$), and sweep
    $\beta \in \{0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8\}$ and $G \in \{2, 4, 16\}$ for
    \texttt{grpo}, \texttt{mean-only} and \texttt{rloo}, with plain SGD.
    We solve Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixedpoint} numerically for each setting and
    run each configuration with both a tabular policy, where
    \cref{prop:finiteG} applies exactly, and the MLP. Hypotheses:
    (i) \texttt{mean-only} and \texttt{rloo} converge to their explicit
    fixed points, which validates the harness; (ii) \texttt{grpo}
    converges to the finite-$G$ fixed point $p^\ast_G$ rather than to
    Mroueh's $G \to \infty$ value, with the gap largest at small $G$ and
    small $\pref$; (iii) at $G = 2$, \texttt{grpo} and \texttt{rloo}
    reach the same success rate; (iv) at $\pref = 10^{-3}$, small-step
    SGD settles at the smallest fixed point even where Mroueh's map
    oscillates or escapes to $p \approx 1$, and raising the learning rate
    moves SGD toward the map's behaviour, which links E2 to E3. The gap
    between the tabular policy and the MLP measures the effect of function
    approximation. We repeat (ii) with $L = 2$ to measure the
    factorization gap. Because we compute the KL exactly, we can also
    measure how much of each update comes from the KL term on groups with
    all-equal rewards, one of the failure modes listed by
    \citet{ding2026klmisfires}.

  \item[E3] \textbf{Step-size threshold~\citep{suk2025gradientgap}.} Start
    every run from the same $p_0$ and sweep the SGD learning rate over a
    log grid. Without a KL penalty, the \texttt{grpo}, \texttt{mean-only}
    and \texttt{rloo} advantages sum to zero within a group, so one step can
    only raise the logits of the correct tokens and lower those of sampled
    wrong ones. On the single-solution task, a policy with separate logits
    for each prompt and position therefore cannot lose success at any
    learning rate, and any threshold
    must come from parameters shared across prompts and positions. E3
    therefore uses the MLP, with the tabular policy as a control that
    should show no threshold. \emph{Fixed length:} for $L \in \{1, 2, 4, 8\}$ with summed
    log-probabilities, we record the largest learning rate $\bar\eta(L)$
    that still improves on $p_0$. With a fixed $L$, averaging the
    log-probabilities over tokens only rescales the learning rate by
    $1/L$, so this arm tests the predicted $\bar\eta \propto 1/L$ scaling
    rather than comparing normalizations. \emph{Mixed length:} prompts
    with $L(x) \in \{2, \dots, 8\}$ share one learning rate, and we
    sweep the length weighting $f_\alpha(L) = L^{\alpha-1}$ of
    \citet{ding2026lengthimpossibility} over $\alpha \in \{0, 0.5, 1\}$,
    from per-response $1/|y|$ averaging (GRPO, $\alpha = 0$) to summed
    log-probabilities ($\alpha = 1$). Dr.~GRPO's constant $1/L_{\max}$
    normalizer equals the summed loss with the learning rate divided by
    $L_{\max}$, so the learning-rate sweep covers it. We run the sweep for
    both \texttt{grpo} and \texttt{mean-only}, since
    \citet{ge2026normalization} predict that std-normalization changes the
    effective step size. Hypotheses: there is a narrow
    learning-rate range where success first rises and then stalls or
    collapses; $\bar\eta$ shrinks roughly as $1/L$ and as $p_0$ grows
    (through $1-J$); in the mixed-length task, the summed loss has its
    threshold set by the longest prompts, so short prompts learn slowly at
    any stable learning rate, while per-response averaging equalizes the
    thresholds across lengths.
    Within a prompt, all responses have the same length, so the
    response-length bias that Dr.~GRPO targets is absent and only the
    step-size effect remains.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Extensions (if time permits).}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em, labelindent=0em]
  \item[E4] \textbf{Diversity under pass@$k$
    shaping~\citep{thrampoulidis2026passk}.} On the multi-solution task,
    compare \texttt{grpo} with pass@$k$ advantages ($k = 4, 8$, with
    $G = 16 \ge k$), the focal-style weighting of
    F-GRPO~\citep{plyusov2026fgrpo}, and updating only on prompts with no
    observed success~\citep{yuan2026overtraining}. We expect higher entropy
    and pass@16 at some cost to pass@1, and test three further predictions:
    a non-monotonic effect of $G$ on the mass of rare correct
    responses~\citep{plyusov2026fgrpo}; the entropy--performance law of
    \citet{cui2025entropy}, whose driving covariance we can compute
    exactly; and the crossover of \citet{yue2025doesrl}, where the trained
    policy overtakes the base model at small $k$ and falls behind it at
    large $k$.
  \item[E5] \textbf{Escaping very sparse rewards.} At $L = 6$,
    $p_0 = 4^{-6} \approx 2.4 \times 10^{-4}$ (where
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative} predicts almost no signal), compare a very
    large $G$~\citep{hu2025brorl}, mixing in easier prompts, a curriculum
    over $L$, sampling prompts in proportion to
    $p(1-p)$~\citep{foster2025learnability} or to
    $\phi(p, G)$~\citep[cf.][]{sha2026thinkprior,okonkwo2026arcus}, and the
    fixed-reference advantage $A = 2r - 1$, which still learns from
    all-wrong groups~\citep{nie2026starvation}. In our task the exact $p$
    lets us run the prompt-selection arms with the true difficulty, an
    upper bound on what an estimated difficulty can achieve.
  \item[E6] \textbf{Small-LLM check.} We first reproduce the experiment of
    \citet[App.~A]{mroueh2025rlvr}, which uses only public components:
    Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct~\citep{qwen2025qwen25} on
    GSM8K~\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, trained with TRL's
    GRPOTrainer~\citep{vonwerra2020trl} and vLLM, $G = 16$,
    learning rate $5 \times 10^{-6}$, $\beta = 0.1$, a 200-token context,
    and sampling temperature 0.1. Its reported success rises from 21\% to
    37.5\%. We then
    repeat E1 on the LLM. We estimate each training prompt's success rate
    from 16 samples of the base model, bucket prompts at
    $p \approx \{0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8\}$, and run
    $G \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$ within each bucket (2 seeds, about 200 steps),
    logging the informative-group fraction against
    Eq.~\eqref{eq:informative}. We add a random-reward control
    run~\citep{shao2025spurious} and plot pass@$k$ of the base and trained
    models up to $k = 256$ to check for the crossover of
    \citet{yue2025doesrl}; \citet{tan2025scaling} report reference curves
    for Qwen2.5-0.5B. \texttt{mean-only} is TRL's
    \texttt{scale\_rewards=False}; for E4 we use verl's built-in pass@$k$
    advantage or a small subclass of the TRL trainer. If GSM8K has too few
    prompts in the hard buckets, we use MATH~\citep{hendrycks2021math}
    difficulty levels 1--5 instead. Runs use 40\,GB MIG slices of H100
    GPUs on the DRAC Fir cluster. We will time a pilot run before
    committing; at an assumed $\sim$2 GPU-hours per run, the 32-run grid
    costs about 3 GPU-days. We drop the multiple-choice variant of our
    earlier plan because its chance level ($\ge 25\%$) cannot reach the
    sparse regime.
\end{description}

\section{Proof of \texorpdfstring{\cref{prop:finiteG}}{the Proposition}}
\label{app:proof}

Fix a prompt and write $\pi = \pi_\theta(\cdot \mid x)$ and
$p = \sum_{y : r(y) = 1} \pi(y)$. The advantages are computed from
sampled rewards and are not differentiated, so the expected update
direction of the reward term is
$g = \E\bigl[\tfrac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G} A_i \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_i)\bigr]$.
The group is exchangeable, so $g = \E[A_1 \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y_1)]$.
Conditional on $y_1$, the other responses are independent of $y_1$, so
$\E[A_1 \mid y_1]$ depends on $y_1$ only through $r(y_1)$, and equals
$w(1) = a^+_G(p)$ or $w(0) = -a^-_G(p)$. Hence
\[
  g = \sum_y \pi(y)\, w(r(y))\, \nabla_\theta \log \pi(y)
    = \nabla_\theta \sum_y \pi_\theta(y)\, w(r(y)) \Big|_{w \text{ fixed}} .
\]
Adding the exact KL penalty, a stationary point is a stationary point of
$F(\pi) = \sum_y \pi(y) w(r(y)) - \beta\, \KL(\pi \,\|\, \piref)$ at fixed
$w$. When the policy class contains every distribution with full support
(a softmax over all responses), $\nabla_\theta F = 0$ if and only if
$w(r(y)) - \beta\bigl(\log\tfrac{\pi(y)}{\piref(y)} + 1\bigr)$ is constant
in $y$, i.e.\ $\pi(y) \propto \piref(y) \exp(w(r(y))/\beta)$. Summing
over correct and incorrect responses,
\[
  \frac{p}{1-p} = \frac{\pref}{1-\pref}
    \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{a^+_G(p) + a^-_G(p)}{\beta}\Bigr),
\]
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixedpoint}, with $p$ evaluated at the stationary
point itself.

For \texttt{mean-only}, $A_1 = r_1 - \frac{1}{G}\sum_i r_i$, so
$a^+_G = 1 - \frac{1 + (G-1)p}{G} = \frac{G-1}{G}(1-p)$ and
$a^-_G = \frac{G-1}{G}p$, giving $\Omega_G = (G-1)/G$. For \texttt{rloo},
$A_1 = r_1 - \frac{1}{G-1}\sum_{i \ge 2} r_i$, so $a^+_G = 1-p$,
$a^-_G = p$ and $\Omega_G = 1$. For \texttt{grpo} with $G = 2$, the only
informative group has rewards $\{0, 1\}$ and advantages
$\pm \tfrac{1}{2}/\sqrt{\tfrac14 + \epsilon} \approx \pm 1$, so
$a^+_2 \approx 1-p$, $a^-_2 \approx p$ and $\Omega_2 \approx 1$. As
$G \to \infty$, $j/G \to p$ in probability, so
$a^+_G \to (1-p)/\sqrt{p(1-p)+\epsilon}$ and
$a^-_G \to p/\sqrt{p(1-p)+\epsilon}$ by bounded convergence, and
$\Omega_G \to (p(1-p)+\epsilon)^{-1/2}$, the weight in the recurrence of
\citet{mroueh2025rlvr}. If the implementation uses the Bessel-corrected
standard deviation, replace $\frac{c}{G}(1-\frac{c}{G})$ in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:omegaG} by $\frac{c(G-c)}{G(G-1)}$. \hfill$\square$

\paragraph{Derivation of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow}.}
Take $L = 1$, $K$ answers with one correct answer $c$, and logits
$\ell_k$, so $\pi_k \propto e^{\ell_k}$. Write
$g_k = w(r_k) - \beta(\log\frac{\pi_k}{\pi_{\mathrm{ref},k}} + 1)$ and
$\bar g = \sum_k \pi_k g_k$. The expected-gradient flow on $F$ is
$\dot\ell_k = \pi_k (g_k - \bar g)$. With a zero-initialized head, the
$K-1$ wrong answers start with equal logits and receive equal updates,
so they stay equal, each with probability
$\pi_w = (1-p)/(K-1)$. Let $\Delta = g_c - g_w$. Then
$\dot\ell_c = p(1-p)\Delta$ and $\dot\ell_w = -p\,\pi_w \Delta$. Using
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref},w} = (1-\pref)/(K-1)$,
$\Delta = a^+_G + a^-_G - \beta(z - \logit \pref)$, where
$z = \logit p = \ell_c - \ell_w - \log(K-1)$. Hence
$\dot z = \dot\ell_c - \dot\ell_w = \kappa\,\Delta$ with
$\kappa = p(1-p) + p\,\pi_w > 0$, which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:flow}. A fixed
point is stable when $\partial\Delta/\partial z < 0$, i.e.\ when
$\Omega_G'(p^\ast)\,p^\ast(1-p^\ast) < \beta$. Mroueh's map
$z_n = \logit\pref + \Omega(\sigma(z_{n-1}))/\beta$ has slope
$s = \Omega'(p^\ast)\,p^\ast(1-p^\ast)/\beta$ at a fixed point and is
stable when $|s| < 1$. The two conditions agree when $s > 0$; when
$s < -1$, the map oscillates but the flow is still stable.
